This page collects the course-wide expectations that would otherwise be
repeated on every lecture and assessment page. It doesn't set institutional
policy — for anything about special consideration, extensions on medical or
compassionate grounds, or formal academic-integrity procedures, follow your
enrolling institution's own policy and contact the teaching team early.

## Attendance and participation

Each week pairs a lecture page with a short interactive experiment built
around that week's psychological question. There's no roll call built into
this site, but "participating" in a week means actually working through
both: reading the lecture, running the experiment yourself rather than just
reading about it, and thinking about the reflection prompt at the end before
moving on. The experiments are designed to be experienced first-hand — the
argument each week makes depends on you having tried it, not just read a
description of it.

## Weekly learning expectations

A normal week's work is: read the lecture page for that week, work through
its interactive experiment, and read the "What just happened?" and
"Reflection" sections that follow — they explain what the experiment can and
can't tell you, and connect it back to that week's question. Lectures build
on each other ("Previously on…" and "Next episode" call back and forward
between weeks) so it's worth keeping roughly to the [weekly
schedule](/sessions/) rather than saving them all up for one sitting.

## Lecture and slide use

Every week's full content lives on its own [lecture page](/lectures/), and
most weeks also have an in-class slide deck linked from that page ("Open the
slides"). The slides are a condensed companion for the session itself, not a
replacement for the lecture page — the page carries the full argument, the
worked examples and the experiment; the deck is what gets projected. Course
materials are shared under this site's licence
(CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0), so you're free to revisit, reuse and adapt them
non-commercially with attribution.

## Interactive experiment participation

The experiments are designed to run locally in your browser and are intended
as interactive demonstrations rather than data-collection studies. Treat
each one the way its own "What this demonstration can't tell you" note (at
the end of every experiment) asks you to: as a small, illustrative
demonstration of a mechanism, run once by one visitor — not a diagnostic
test of you, and not a controlled study. What matters is noticing the
mechanism, not the score or outcome you get.

<Callout type="tip">
  If an experiment doesn't load, doesn't respond well to a keyboard or
  screen reader, or otherwise doesn't work for you, that's a bug in the
  site, not a requirement you're expected to work around — see
  [Accessibility and support](#accessibility-and-support) below.
</Callout>

## Assessment and submission expectations

Each assessment's brief, due date and weighting is published on the
[Assessment](/assessments/) page rather than repeated here, so there's a
single place they can be kept correct. The general expectation is the same
for every piece of work: submit what the brief asks for, by the date it
states, in the format it names, and check that page rather than a lecture or
this one for the current figures — this page won't be updated in step with
assessment changes.

## Academic integrity and responsible use of AI

Work you submit should represent your own understanding and your own
writing. Where an assessment brief allows the use of AI tools, follow that
brief's own instructions on how to use and disclose them — the expectations
can reasonably differ between a reflective piece and a technical build, so
the brief on the [Assessment](/assessments/) page is the authority, not a
blanket rule here. If a brief doesn't say, ask the teaching team before you
submit rather than after.

## Accessibility and support

If anything on this site — a lecture page, a slide deck, or an interactive
experiment — is hard to use, that's worth raising rather than working
around. The [People](/people/) page lists how to reach each member of the
teaching team and when to use which channel: email for course-level
questions that can't wait, or the weekly session for anything that can. If
you're not sure who to ask, start with the convenor.

## Course communication expectations

The weekly session is the fastest way to get an answer to most questions.
For anything that can't wait until then, email the relevant person listed on
the [People](/people/) page directly, rather than a general course inbox —
this course doesn't have one. Keep questions about a specific week's content
attached to that week where you can; it makes them easier to answer and
easier for a later student to find.
