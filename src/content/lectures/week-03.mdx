## This week's question

Week 1 already named "unfinished information" as one reason it's hard to
stop at the end of an episode. This week stays on that exact mechanism and
asks a sharper question: unfinished *how*? Not every open ending pulls the
same amount. A scene can end with the plot unresolved and barely register,
or end on almost nothing happening and be unbearable to leave. What decides
which one you get isn't how much *story* is left — it's how much of a
specific **question** is left hanging in the air.

## What a cliffhanger actually withholds

A cliffhanger's power doesn't come from stopping the story early. Plenty of
scenes stop early and nobody minds. It comes from **posing a question
explicitly and then refusing to answer it** — a door opens and the show cuts
before showing who's there, a sentence gets cut off mid-word. The unresolved
plot is almost incidental; what's doing the pulling is a mind that now has an
open loop it didn't ask for and can't close on its own.

This is the same open-loop effect that makes an interrupted task nag at you
more than a finished one, aimed at fiction on purpose. A show that ends
cleanly gives you nothing to resolve, so there's nothing pulling you into the
next episode except ordinary interest. A show that ends on an explicit,
unanswered question hands you a loop that only closes one way: pressing
play again.

## A familiar scene

An episode ends with a character opening a door, one line of dialogue
suggesting the person on the other side is someone the story has spent the
whole season building toward — and then the screen cuts to the credits
before showing who it actually is. You already know, rationally, that the
answer is sitting one click away and isn't going anywhere. That knowledge
doesn't loosen the pull at all. The open loop isn't about scarcity of the
answer; it's about the fact that a specific question was asked out loud and
then deliberately left open.

## What just happened?

Each ending on that list was written to withhold a different amount of
information on purpose — from a scene that answers its own question outright,
through one that raises a question but keeps it open, to one that
interrupts an answer mid-delivery. That's this page's own illustrative
ordering, not a scored "correct" ranking: it's a teaching label for how
explicitly each ending poses and withholds a question, not a measurement of
anything about you.

If your ranking mostly agreed with that order, that's the mechanism showing
up in miniature: the more explicitly a question gets asked and then denied
an answer, the stronger the open loop. If it didn't agree — worth
noticing too. A single ranking from a single visitor isn't evidence about
how "susceptible to cliffhangers" you are; people vary a great deal in which
specific kind of unresolved question bothers them, and this is one visitor
ranking four made-up lines once, not a validated instrument.

## Reflection

Think of a cliffhanger that actually got you to watch the next episode
immediately, and one that didn't work on you at all — you could shrug and
walk away. Using "how explicit and specific was the open question" as the
lens instead of "how good was the writing," what's different between those
two examples?
