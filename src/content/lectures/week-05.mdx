## This week's question

Imagine two people who each check for a new episode of a show six times, and
each gets rewarded with "yes, it's out" exactly twice. By any measure of
quantity, they had identical experiences. Ask them how compelling it felt to
keep checking, though, and the answers can be completely different. The
number of rewards was never the variable that mattered — **when** they
landed was.

## Reliable versus unpredictable

A reward that arrives on a fixed, learnable rhythm gets treated by the mind
as basically solved: once the pattern is known, there's no tension left in
checking, because checking has stopped being informative. A reward that
arrives on an irregular, unpredictable rhythm never resolves that way. Every
single check stays genuinely informative — this could be the one — and that
uncertainty is what keeps checking behaviour going long after a predictable
schedule would have been abandoned as "solved."

This is the same mechanism behind why refreshing a feed, checking for a
notification, or re-opening an app "just in case" can feel compelling in a
way that checking something on a known schedule (like a weekly episode
release) usually doesn't. The unpredictability isn't a side effect of the
reward being rare — it's doing the actual psychological work, independent of
how often the reward shows up.

## A familiar scene

Two habits, one person: checking the mail, which arrives at roughly the same
time most days, and refreshing a social feed, which sometimes has something
new after ten seconds and sometimes after twenty minutes. Nobody obsessively
re-checks the mailbox once they've learned its schedule. Plenty of people
re-check a feed dozens of times in an hour that yields the same average
number of "new" moments the mailbox does in a week. The difference was never
about how much reward showed up — it's about not being able to predict when.

## What just happened?

- **The fixed schedule** rewarded you on a perfectly regular rhythm — once
  the pattern became apparent, later checks stopped being genuinely
  uncertain.
- **The variable schedule** rewarded you the same total number of times, on
  an irregular rhythm that never resolved into a learnable pattern — every
  check stayed uncertain until the moment it landed.

Both patterns above were fixed in advance for this demonstration, not live
random draws — they're illustrative teaching patterns, not a real
probability system. If checking felt more compelling, harder to stop, or
more attention-grabbing during the variable round even though the total
payoff was identical, that's this week's mechanism showing up directly:
unpredictability, not quantity, is what keeps a checking habit alive.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** two six-check rounds from one
visitor aren't a controlled study, the rounds always ran in the same order,
and one person's reaction to two made-up patterns is not evidence about your
own vulnerability to unpredictable rewards specifically. What repeated,
controlled studies of reinforcement schedules (not this demo) actually show
is that variable schedules sustain behaviour longer on average across many
subjects — not that this round proved something stable about you personally.

## Reflection

Think of a habit you keep returning to that has an unpredictable payoff —
checking something, refreshing something, hoping for a particular outcome.
If that same payoff arrived on a totally predictable schedule instead, same
total amount, would you still check as often? What does your honest answer
say about which part was actually pulling you back?
