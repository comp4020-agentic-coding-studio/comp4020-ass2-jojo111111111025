## This week's question

Every earlier week in this course has assumed the same shape: watching
continues because something makes *continuing* attractive — effort, an open
question, a badge. Autoplay is a different kind of design decision entirely.
It doesn't need to make continuing attractive. It only needs to make
*stopping* into a task, and let default behaviour do the rest.

## Defaults do work that persuasion doesn't have to

Behavioural research on defaults is blunt about this: whatever happens when
a person does nothing tends to keep happening, regardless of whether they'd
have chosen it if asked directly. This isn't about laziness or lack of
willpower — reversing a default takes a deliberate act, and deliberate acts
compete with everything else going on in a moment (tiredness, distraction, a
countdown that's already halfway gone). Every one of those small costs makes
"just let it happen" slightly more likely to win, one episode at a time.

Compare that to Week 1's friction condition, where *both* options — continue
and stop — required a click, and a temporary disable on one button was
enough to nudge behaviour. Autoplay goes a step further: continuing needs no
action whatsoever. The only thing that ever requires effort is the option
the interface would prefer you not take.

## A familiar scene

A ten-second countdown appears at the bottom of the screen: "Next episode
starts automatically." The button that would stop it is right there, clearly
visible, not hidden or disabled — nothing is stopping you from clicking it
except the fact that not clicking anything is *also* an option, and it's the
one that requires nothing from you at all. Ten seconds later, the next
episode has already started, and no decision ever consciously got made.

## The interactive experiment: The Ten-Second Window

Below are three rounds. In each one, something is about to play
automatically after a real countdown — the countdown is exactly the same
length every time. Some rounds tell you up front that it's about to happen;
some don't. Some make the "stop" button available right away; one makes you
wait a moment before it appears. Go with what actually happens in the
moment, not what you'd plan to do in the abstract.

## What just happened?

- **Round 1 was the baseline.** The stop control was visible and available
  the entire time, and the countdown was announced clearly. Whatever
  happened here is what the other two rounds are compared against.
- **Round 2 buried the control**, not the countdown. The same real length of
  time ran, announced the same way — but the button that would stop it
  stayed unreachable for the first couple of seconds. If autoplay won this
  round when it didn't win round 1, that's the mechanism in miniature: the
  window didn't get shorter, reaching the control just cost more of it.
- **Round 3 stayed silent.** The stop control was just as reachable as
  round 1, immediately — but nothing announced up front that anything was
  about to happen automatically at all. If autoplay won here, that isolates
  a second, separate lever from round 2's: not "can you reach the button,"
  but "did you even know there was a countdown to interrupt."

That's this week's question, answered in miniature: the countdown itself
never changed across all three rounds. What changed was how much it cost, in
attention or reach, to exercise the option that was technically available
the entire time.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** three rounds from one visitor
say nothing about your own self-control or your susceptibility to autoplay
specifically — the rounds ran in a fixed order, the "silent" round loses some
of its effect for anyone reading this debrief before playing it, and one
person's outcome is not a controlled comparison. What repeated,
controlled studies of default effects (not this demo) actually show is that
burying or delaying an opt-out shifts outcomes on average across many
people — not that any one round's result says something stable about you.

## Reflection

Think about the last time you noticed an episode had started playing before
you'd decided to watch it. In that moment, was the "stop" option genuinely
unreachable — or was it reachable, but not something you were thinking about
until after the decision had already been made for you?
