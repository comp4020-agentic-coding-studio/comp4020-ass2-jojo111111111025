## This week's question

Ten weeks in, a pattern should be obvious: cliffhangers, autoplay,
intermittent rewards, narrowing recommendations, social cues, and an
easy-to-ignore sleep cost don't just each nudge one decision — strung
together, they form a loop, where the end of one mechanism's effect is the
start of the next one's. Asking "how do I stop watching" treats that loop as
a single wall to push against. A more useful question treats it as a
sequence with more than one point along it: **where in that cycle does a
small interruption actually change what happens next?**

## The same interruption, different places

Picture the loop as five fixed stages, repeating: a **cue** draws attention
toward what's next, an **episode** plays, **autoplay** starts counting down
before anyone has decided anything, a **reward** lands — satisfying, but
with one thread left open — and a **recommendation** appears, which is
itself the next cue. Nothing about this loop requires four, or forty, laps.
It just keeps going until something interrupts it.

Here's the part that's easy to miss: the *same* interruption — declining an
autoplay countdown instead of letting it run — does very different work
depending on where in the loop it happens. Interrupt during the very first
autoplay countdown, and the loop never gets a chance to repeat. Interrupt
during the fourth or fifth countdown instead, and the interruption still
works — the loop still stops — but only after several full laps have
already played out. Same action, same mechanism, very different outcome,
purely because of timing. That distinction matters more than it sounds: a
person who "finally stopped" after five episodes used exactly the same tool
as a person who stopped after one. The difference wasn't willpower. It was
where the interruption landed in an already-turning cycle.

## A familiar scene

It's not that you never try to stop. It's that "I'll stop after this one"
gets said at the *reward* stage — right when a resolution has just landed
and a new recommendation is already glowing on screen — instead of at the
*autoplay* stage, before the next episode has even started counting down.
By the time the intention to stop shows up, the loop has already moved past
the cheapest place to interrupt it.

## What just happened?

All three runs used exactly the same loop, the same five stages, and the
same interruption — declining autoplay. Nothing about the mechanism itself
changed between runs. What changed was purely *when* it happened: early in
the cycle, late in the cycle, or not at all. That's the whole demonstration,
compressed into three deterministic runs of a made-up four-lap cap.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** a fixed, illustrative loop run
three predetermined ways by one visitor isn't evidence about your own
capacity to interrupt a real viewing habit, and it isn't a claim that
"stopping late" reflects some personal shortfall. The point is structural:
an intervention placed earlier in a reinforcing cycle costs less than the
same intervention placed later, regardless of who is doing the
interrupting. Reading a late stop as a character flaw — instead of as a
timing question — is exactly the framing this page is trying to replace.

## Reflection

Think of a real loop you've been in — not necessarily watching — where you
eventually did interrupt yourself, just later than you'd have liked: another
tab, another scroll, another purchase. If you could replay it and place the
exact same interruption you actually used, but earlier in the cycle, what
would have had to be different for that to be possible? Was the obstacle
really weaker willpower, or was it that the earlier moment didn't feel like
a decision point at all?
