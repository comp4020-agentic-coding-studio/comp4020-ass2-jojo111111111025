## This week's question

Week 1 started after the choice was already made — you were watching, and
something about the ending of an episode made it hard to stop. This week
goes one step earlier, to the moment before any of that applies: **what
actually gets a show onto the screen in the first place?**

It's tempting to answer "I chose it because it looked good" and stop there.
But most of the time, you knew almost nothing about the show when you
pressed play. Something else was doing the deciding.

## Two things that decide before you do

Neither of these requires the show to be good, or even for you to know
anything real about it.

The first is **social proof**. A badge that says a show is popular, highly
matched, or trending doesn't tell you anything about the show's actual
content — it tells you what other people already did. People reliably treat
"other people picked this" as a shortcut for "this is worth picking," even
when they have no idea who those other people are or why they picked it.
It's a cue about the crowd, not about the plot.

The second is the same **unfinished information** effect from last week,
aimed at a different moment. Instead of an unresolved scene keeping you from
stopping, it's an unresolved *description* — a synopsis that poses a
question instead of answering one — that gets you to start. "A family
comedy" tells you what you're getting. "She answers the door, and it's her,
twenty years younger" tells you there's something you don't have yet, and
that's the part that pulls.

Neither mechanism has anything to do with whether the show is actually good.
That's the point: the decision to press play is much more manipulable than
it feels from the inside.

## A familiar scene

You open a streaming app with no particular show in mind. Dozens of tiles
are ready before you've typed anything into search. Within a few seconds —
often less time than it took to read this sentence — you've tapped one. Ask
yourself honestly, afterward, why that one: the honest answer is rarely "I
read all the descriptions and picked the best match." It's closer to "that
one had a badge" or "that description made me want to know what happens."

## What just happened?

- **Round 1 was the baseline — your starting preference.** All three shows
  looked the same: no badges, plain descriptions. Whatever you picked here
  is the reading with nothing added, and it's what rounds 2 and 3 are
  measured against, not a "correct" answer.
- **Round 2 added a social-proof badge** to one show. The badge says nothing
  about the show's content, only that (supposedly) other people already
  picked it — this is the condition demonstrating that popularity
  information alone can move a choice, independent of the options'
  descriptions.
- **Round 3 replaced one show's description with an unresolved detail**
  instead of a plot summary — the same curiosity gap Week 1 used to keep
  people watching, here demonstrating that an unresolved question can hand
  you a reason to start a show you otherwise know nothing about.

If you picked the cued show in round 2 or 3, that's the mechanism working —
not evidence that you're unusually susceptible to badges or blurbs. One
click, on one browse screen, tells you almost nothing about your own
tendencies; the cued option even sits in a different slot each round so this
demo isn't secretly measuring "clicks the middle tile." What repeated
studies (not this three-click demo) actually show is that both cues shift
group-level choice rates — not that any individual choice was made *for*
you.

That's this week's question, answered in miniature: "why do we press play"
rarely has "because of the show" as its real answer. Compare what moved
between your three picks — the shows never changed, so whatever moved was
the cue sitting next to them.

## Reflection

Think about the last show you started without a recommendation from a
person you know — something you picked cold, off a menu. Was there a badge,
a ranking, a "top 10," or a description that left something open? If you
take that cue away in your memory of the moment, would you have still
picked the same show — or is it possible you were choosing the cue, and the
show just came attached to it?
