## This week's question

A recommendation feed never has to say no to you. It doesn't need to block a
genre or remove an option to shape what you watch — it only needs to notice
what you already picked, and quietly offer you more of that next time. Do
five small, individually reasonable picks in a row change what you're even
being shown by the sixth?

## Feedback loops don't need intent to narrow

The mechanism here doesn't require a feed to be trying to trap you. A simple
rule — "show more of what this person picked before" — is enough on its own,
because every pick becomes an input to the next round's options, and the
next round's options shape what's *available* to pick from. There's no step
where variety gets deliberately removed. There's just a rule that responds
to your last pick, applied repeatedly, and repetition is what does the
narrowing.

This is worth separating clearly from taste. Liking comedies more than
documentaries and ending up shown mostly comedies looks the same from the
outside as a feed narrowing on its own momentum, independent of whether your
underlying interest ever actually changed. The two get tangled together
constantly — genuine preference feeding a system that then amplifies exactly
that preference until it looks less like a preference and more like the
whole menu.

## A familiar scene

You watch one workplace comedy on a whim, on a slow evening. Within a few
sessions, your home screen is visibly heavier on comedies than it used to
be — not because you told it your genre preferences, and not because you
watched anything else in between. One pick, repeated back to you as more
of the same, is often all it takes to notice the shape of the feed itself
start to shift.

## The interactive experiment: The Narrowing Feed

Below is a small, five-round version of that loop. Each round shows four
shows from four categories; pick one. The next round's mix updates based
only on what you just picked, following a fixed, simplified rule — not a
real streaming algorithm, just enough of the same shape to make the
mechanism visible.

## What just happened?

Every round's tile mix was generated purely from your own pick history, fed
through a fixed weighting rule set in advance — nothing here adapted to
guess your "real" taste, and no live model or randomness was involved. If
the categories on screen narrowed noticeably by round five, that's the
mechanism working exactly as designed: a handful of individually small picks
compounding into a visibly smaller menu, with no single step where anyone
decided to remove an option.

If you deliberately varied your picks across categories, you likely saw the
mix stay closer to its starting diversity — worth noticing, since it shows
the narrowing isn't inevitable, only a predictable response to a *pattern*
of picks, not to any one pick in isolation.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** a four-category, five-round toy
catalogue with a fixed formula is a teaching simplification, not the
algorithm any real streaming platform actually runs, and your reaction to it
says nothing about your real-world viewing habits or how "in a bubble" you
personally are. What it can show, reliably, is the shape of the mechanism —
that this kind of feedback loop narrows a feed mechanically, given nothing
more than a rule and repetition.

## Reflection

Think about your own most-used streaming or video app's home screen right
now. If you tried to remember what it looked like the very first time you
used it, would you expect it to look meaningfully different from today's —
and if so, was that difference something you asked for, or something that
accumulated from picks you made without thinking about where they'd lead?
