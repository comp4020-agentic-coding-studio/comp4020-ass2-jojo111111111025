## This week's question

Week 7 showed what happens to a running total when nothing shows it to you.
This week asks the mirror-image question: what happens when the cost of "one
more episode" is shown, clearly, in a unit you already understand — like
sleep, measured against a wake-up time that isn't moving?

## A fixed cost is easy to underweight anyway

Even when a cost is visible, it doesn't automatically feel as heavy as an
equivalent cost that arrives all at once. Losing forty-two minutes of sleep
in a single visible chunk, over and over, tends to get discounted against
whatever benefit is happening right now — the current episode, which is
vivid and immediate — compared to tomorrow morning's tiredness, which is
abstract and not happening yet. This is the same asymmetry behind why future
costs in general get underweighted relative to present rewards: the reward
is here; the cost is a forecast.

Making the cost numerically explicit doesn't erase that asymmetry. It just
removes one excuse for it — "I didn't realize how late it was" stops being
available once a running number is sitting right there the whole time. What's
left, once that excuse is gone, is a cleaner look at whether visibility
alone is enough to change the decision, or whether the pull of "one more"
survives even a cost you can literally watch draining away.

## A familiar scene

It's 11pm, an alarm is set for 7am, and one more 42-minute episode is
tempting. If nothing on screen ever mentions the time, "one more" doesn't
feel like a decision about sleep at all — it feels like a decision about
whether the show is good enough to keep watching, full stop. Put a visible
countdown to the alarm right next to the play button instead, and the
decision doesn't become easier, exactly — but it becomes a decision about
*something*, instead of a decision that quietly wasn't being framed as a
trade-off at all.

## What just happened?

The cost never changed round to round — every episode you watched spent the
exact same fixed amount, and the remaining budget was visible the entire
time, not revealed only at the end the way Week 7's hidden total was. That
difference is the point: this week isn't testing whether a cost exists,
it's testing whether *seeing* it changes anything about whether you keep
paying it.

If you kept watching well past the point where the remaining bar looked
thin, that's worth sitting with honestly — it suggests visibility alone
isn't the same as visibility mattering. If you stopped earlier because the
number made the trade-off concrete, that's the opposite finding, and just as
real. Neither result is the "correct" one; the demonstration exists to
surface which pattern your own decision followed, once excuses about not
knowing the cost were taken off the table.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** a made-up 8-hour budget and a
fixed 42-minute cost are illustrative numbers for this page, not a real
measurement of your sleep needs or your actual viewing habits, and one
session is not a controlled study of how you trade sleep against
entertainment in general. What matters here is the mechanism made visible,
not a diagnosis of your own habits from a single run.

## Reflection

Think of a real night where you traded sleep for "one more" with a wake-up
time you already knew. If you'd had a running number like the one above,
visible the whole time, do you honestly think it would have changed your
choice — or would you have kept going anyway, with the number just making
you feel slightly worse about a decision you'd already made?
