## This week's question

Week 1 was about the moment right after one episode ends — the specific
decision to watch one more or not. That decision doesn't stop happening once
you've made it once. A binge is the same decision, made again and again,
often dozens of times in an evening. What this week asks isn't "why do you
make that decision" — it's "why does nobody, including you, ever seem to be
adding it up while it's happening?"

## Nothing resets, and nothing announces the total

Each individual "one more episode" decision is small and locally reasonable —
you're already watching, you already like it, one more costs comparatively
little on top of what you've already spent. What compounds across a binge
isn't any single decision getting worse; it's that **no decision in the
sequence ever has to reckon with the running total**, because nothing in the
experience shows you one. Each episode ends by cueing the next episode, not
by cueing a tally of how many you've already watched.

Contrast this with almost anything else you'd track deliberately — money
spent, calories eaten, time on a timer. Those all have a visible running
number specifically so a threshold can register before it's crossed by a lot.
A binge session is designed the opposite way: every individual step forward
is visible, but the sum of those steps is not, until you stop and look back
at it from the outside.

## A familiar scene

You sit down meaning to watch "an episode or two." Four hours later you
surface, and the honest answer to "how many did you actually watch" takes a
moment to work out — not because the number is secret, but because nothing
during those four hours ever asked you to hold it in mind. Compare that to
being asked, after every single episode, "you've now watched six — still
going?" That version of the same evening would almost certainly have ended
sooner. The episodes didn't change. Only whether the count was ever visible
did.

one at a time, or stop whenever you want. The running count is deliberately
not shown while you're deciding — only a small chip appears for each episode

## What just happened?

The number of episodes you watched was tracked the entire time — it was
just never shown to you while you were deciding whether to keep going. If
the revealed total was higher than you expected while you were mid-session,
that's this week's mechanism directly: a hidden running total doesn't slow
anyone down, because there was never a number to react to in the first
place. If it matched what you expected, that's worth noticing too — not
everyone loses track at the same point, or at all, in a single short demo.

The teaser copy also shifted with each episode, leaning more on "you've come
this far" as the count went up. That's the same momentum framing a real
binge often supplies for itself, out loud or not: the more you've already
watched, the more stopping starts to feel like the thing that needs
justifying, not continuing.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** one short session on a made-up
show, with a hard cap this demo needed for practical reasons, is not
evidence about your real binge-watching habits, your self-control, or how
many episodes you "actually" tend to lose track after. What it can show is
the mechanism in isolation — that hiding a running total changes how a
sequence of small decisions feels, independent of what any one person's
personal threshold happens to be.

## Reflection

Think of the last time you were surprised, afterward, by how many episodes
you'd actually watched in one sitting. If a visible counter had been on
screen the entire time — not blocking anything, just present — do you think
you'd have stopped earlier? What does your answer say about whether the
issue was ever really about wanting to keep watching, versus never being
shown a reason to stop?
