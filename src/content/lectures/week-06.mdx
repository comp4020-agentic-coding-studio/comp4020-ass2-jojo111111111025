## This week's question

Ask someone who just finished a three-episode binge how long it took, and
they'll often guess low — sometimes by a lot. Ask someone who spent fifteen
minutes waiting for a delayed bus how long *that* took, and they'll often
guess high. Clocks don't work that way. Something else is doing the
measuring.

## Why time doesn't measure itself

Nobody has a stopwatch running in their head. Instead, a rough estimate of
"how long was that" gets built out of two very different kinds of evidence,
and which one you're stuck with depends on what you were doing at the time.

The first is **how much you had to attend to**. When something keeps giving
you new things to notice — a fast cut, a new detail, a plot turn — most of
your attention goes to *that*, and very little is left over for tracking
duration itself. Less attention spent on timing produces a shorter estimate
afterward, regardless of how much clock time actually passed.

The second is **how little there was to attend to**. When nothing new is
happening, there's nothing competing for your attention, so more of it ends
up spent noticing that time is passing at all — which, perversely, makes the
wait feel *longer*, not shorter. A boring wait doesn't just feel unpleasant;
it feels long specifically because you had nothing else to do but notice it
going by.

Put the two together and you get the binge-watching version of this: a
gripping, event-dense episode leaves you with almost no felt sense of how
long you were watching, so "one more" doesn't feel like it costs anything —
the clock disagrees, but your sense of elapsed time was never asking the
clock in the first place.

## A familiar scene

You start an episode around 9pm intending to watch one. Three episodes and
one deeply eventful hour-and-a-half later, you check the time and it reads
almost midnight — and your honest, gut-level reaction is closer to confusion
than guilt. It didn't *feel* like two and a half hours. Compare that to the
fifteen minutes you spent earlier that same day watching a loading spinner:
objectively a tenth of the time, and it probably felt longer. The show
wasn't tricking you about the clock. Your sense of duration was just built
out of something other than the clock the whole time.

## The interactive experiment: The Guessing Clock

Below are three rounds. Each one runs for exactly the same real length of
time — you won't be told how long, and there's no visible countdown or clock
while it's running. When a round ends on its own, you'll drag a slider to
guess how many seconds you think just passed. Don't count seconds in your
head on purpose; just watch, and guess afterward the way you actually would.

## What just happened?

- **Round 1 was the baseline.** A plain, unchanging scene, run for the
  round's real duration with nothing designed to speed up or slow down how
  it felt. Your guess here is what the other two rounds are compared
  against, not a "correct" answer.
- **Round 2 was the absorbing condition.** The exact same real duration as
  round 1, filled instead with something new to notice every second or so.
  If this round's guess came in lower than round 1's, that's the mechanism
  this week is about: less attention left over for tracking time produces a
  shorter felt duration, even though nothing about the clock changed.
- **Round 3 was the monotonous condition.** Again, the exact same real
  duration — but filled with the same information-free line repeating on a
  slow, steady beat. If this round's guess came in higher than round 1's,
  that's the other half of the mechanism: an empty, repetitive wait leaves
  attention with nothing to do but notice time passing, which inflates the
  estimate.

That's this week's question, answered in miniature: three rounds, identical
real length, three different guesses — the only thing that changed between
them was what your attention had to work with. Scale that up to a real binge
and the eventful, fast-moving stretch of episodes is the "absorbing" round
writ large: not a trick, just very little left over to notice the clock
running.

**What this demonstration can't tell you:** duration estimates like this are
noisy even from the same person on different days, the three rounds always
ran in a fixed order rather than a randomised one, and a single guess from a
single visitor — you — is not evidence about your own attention span, your
self-control, or how "susceptible" you are to losing track of time. What
repeated, controlled studies (not this three-round demo) actually show is
that filled and empty time get judged differently on average across many
people. This page can only make you feel the mechanism once; it can't
measure where your own personal baseline sits.

**An honest limitation of the interaction itself:** the "absorbing"
round's fast-changing on-screen lines aren't announced to a screen reader in
real time — doing that would mean a screen reader interrupting itself every
second, which would be its own kind of unpleasant and would arguably
manipulate a *different* sense than the one this week is about. That's a
genuine gap, not a solved one: a visitor using a screen reader can complete
every round and reach the same final comparison, but doesn't get an
equivalent moment-to-moment experience of the "information-dense" condition
that a sighted visitor gets. Worth naming plainly rather than papering over.

## Reflection

Think of the last time you completely lost track of how long you'd been
watching something, and compare it to the last time a short wait felt
endless. Now that "how much was there to attend to" is named as the
mechanism doing the work in both cases: does it change how you'd explain
either moment to someone else — from "I don't know where the time went" or
"it felt like forever," to something closer to "there was either too much or
too little to notice, and my sense of time went with it"?
